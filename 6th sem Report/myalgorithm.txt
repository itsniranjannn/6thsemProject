1. Content‑Based Filtering – “Similar to what you’re looking at”
What it does:
When you view a product (say, an iPhone), the system finds other products that are similar in category or price.

How it works (simple):

Same category → gets +3 points

Price within 30% range → gets +2 points

Highest points win.

Example:
You are looking at a Samsung phone ($700).

Another phone ($680) → same category (+3), price close (+2) → total 5 → recommended.

A laptop ($1200) → different category (0 points), price not close → total 0 → not recommended.

Why it’s useful:
No need to know anything about the user. Works even for new users.

2. Collaborative Filtering – “People like you also bought…”
What it does:
Finds other users who bought the same products as you, then recommends what they bought that you haven’t.

How it works (simple):

You bought a phone and a case.

System finds 10 other people who also bought that phone and case.

Among those people, the most popular other product is a screen protector.

System recommends screen protector to you.

Example:

User A buys: Laptop, Mouse, Keyboard

User B buys: Laptop, Mouse, USB Hub

You bought: Laptop, Mouse

System sees User A and B are similar to you, so it recommends Keyboard and USB Hub.

Why it’s useful:
Discovers products you didn’t know existed. Very powerful for repeat users.

Fallback:
If you have no purchase history, it shows popular products instead.

3. Popularity‑Based – “What everyone is buying”
What it does:
Simply shows the most purchased, featured, or new products.

Formula in simple words:
Each product gets points:

Every time it’s bought → 2 points

If it’s marked “featured” → +3 points

If it’s “new arrival” → +2 points
Sort by total points, show top ones.

Example:

Product A: bought 10 times (20 points) + featured (3) = 23 points

Product B: bought 5 times (10 points) + new (2) = 12 points

Product A ranks higher.

Why it’s useful:
Perfect for new users, home page, or when other algorithms fail.

4. ML (Cosine Similarity) – “Smart comparison using many features”
What it does:
Compares products using 8 different characteristics at once, like category, price, discount, stock, age, etc. Then calculates a similarity score between 0 and 1.

How it works (very simple):
Imagine each product is a person with 8 personality traits. The system measures how “close” two products are. If the score is above 0.1, it recommends.

The 8 features (you can mention a few):

Category (phone, laptop, etc.)

Price (normalized)

Is it featured?

Is it new?

How much stock is left?

Discount percentage

How old is the product? (newer = better)

Price tier (cheap, medium, expensive)

Example:
iPhone 15 and Samsung S23:

Same category → high similarity

Price close → high similarity

Both new → high similarity

Similarity score = 0.85 → recommended.

Why it’s useful:
More accurate than simple category matching. Catches subtle similarities.

Cache trick:
The system stores these feature vectors for 5 minutes so it doesn’t recompute every time → faster response.

5. Hybrid – “Best of all worlds”
What it does:
Runs ML, content, and popularity algorithms together, removes duplicates, and gives you a mixed list.

How it works (simple):

Ask ML for 5 recommendations

Ask content for 5 recommendations

Ask popularity for 5 recommendations

Combine all, remove same products, pick top 5.

Example:
ML suggests: Phone, Phone Case, Charger
Content suggests: Phone, Earbuds, Charger
Popularity suggests: Phone, Laptop, Mouse
Hybrid result: Phone, Phone Case, Charger, Earbuds, Laptop

Why it’s useful:
No single algorithm is perfect. Hybrid covers more possibilities.

🗣️ How to Answer the Examiner’s Likely Questions
Q: Which algorithm is best?

“None is best alone. ML gives highest accuracy, but hybrid gives diversity. For a new user, we use popularity. For returning user, we use collaborative or hybrid.”

Q: How do you know it works?

“We tested each algorithm with sample data. For example, cosine similarity returned 0.85 for two phones in same category, and 0 for unrelated products. We also did unit tests (show test table).”

Q: Why cosine similarity?

“It’s simple, fast, and works well for high‑dimensional data like product features. We don’t need complex neural networks for this scale.”

Q: What happens if no similar products?

“We have fallbacks. ML falls back to content, collaborative falls back to popularity. The user always sees something.”

Q: Can you give a real example from your project?

“Yes. When you view ‘iPhone 17’ (product ID 7), the ML algorithm finds ‘Samsung S23’ because category matches and price is within 30%. The similarity score was 0.72, so it shows up in ‘You May Also Like’.”
